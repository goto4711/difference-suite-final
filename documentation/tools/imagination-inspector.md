# Imagination Inspector

**Route:** `/imagination-inspector` · **Models:** CLIP ViT-B/32 (zero-shot demographic classification + dataset alignment); SmolLM2-135M text fallback · **Data:** Stable Bias dataset (`stable-bias/professions`) via HuggingFace Dataset Viewer API · **Key dependencies:** `@huggingface/transformers`, custom `GeneratorEngine`, `BiasAnalyzer`, `clipUncertainty` · **Archival site:** Incidental archives · **Try it with:** built-in profession prompts; align against `images/stable_bias/` or `images/animals/`

---

## Keyword Translation

**Imagination: Generativity → Creativity**

Generative AI is the most visible face of deep learning's cultural impact, yet critics argue it is not truly creative — that it cannot distinguish "the possible from the impossible" (Chomsky, Roberts and Watumull, 2023). The Imagination Inspector probes this boundary, asking not what generative models *can* produce but what they systematically *cannot* — and who is rendered absent in the gap.

## Theoretical Context

Behind every synthetic image lies very real data. The project's interest in "incidental archives" — collections that exist as by-products of other activities and were never intended as training material — meets generative AI precisely here: models learn an imagination from scraped corpora whose demographics are skewed, and reproduce those skews as if they were the shape of the world. Documenting what such a model omits is a way of reading its training archive against the grain, surfacing the "selective forgettings" (after Stoler) encoded in its outputs.

## How It Works

Rather than describing hypothetical images, the Inspector retrieves **real Stable Diffusion outputs** from the **Stable Bias dataset** (images across 146 professions, generated by Stable Diffusion v1.4/v2 and DALL·E 2), fetched live through the HuggingFace Dataset Viewer API. For a profession prompt it pulls a grid of generated images and classifies each one's demographics — gender, race, age, setting — using **CLIP zero-shot classification** in a batched call per image. A custom **BiasAnalyzer** tallies the distribution, and an **Absence Report** ("Void Report") names the demographics present in reality but missing from the model's output. An **adjective toggle** controls sampling: *Varied* draws a different adjective per image from the dataset's 21 real adjectives, while *Fixed* holds one constant for controlled comparison, addressing the dataset by block offset because the API cannot filter on compound conditions. A **Dataset Alignment** sidebar uses **CLIP** to match the prompt to the user's own images (or built-in archetypes — CEO, nurse, criminal, professor, terrorist, worker), and a **comparison mode** runs two prompts side by side. For the 146 known professions the tool shows real images; for anything else it falls back to a **SmolLM2-135M** text simulation, explicitly badged as unreliable.

## Methods Setup

The pivotal methodological decision is to work with *real generated images rather than live in-browser generation or pure simulation*. In-browser text-to-image was infeasible at the lightweight scale the suite targets, and a text-only simulation would merely launder the language model's own priors; retrieving the Stable Bias corpus grounds the analysis in actual diffusion outputs whose biases are documented and citable. CLIP *zero-shot* classification is the enabling choice — it can sort images into demographic categories without any task-specific training, which keeps the pipeline light but also, honestly, imports CLIP's own classificatory assumptions, a limit the tool's framing acknowledges rather than hides. The decision to compute and foreground *absence* — the Void Report — is the project's theory made into a metric: presence distributions are common in bias audits, but naming what is *missing* operationalises "erasure" directly. The Varied/Fixed adjective toggle exists to support controlled comparison, and the documentation of *why* Fixed mode uses block-offset addressing (the API's lack of compound filters) is itself a small act of methodological transparency. The SmolLM2 fallback is retained but badged unreliable, an honest marker of where the tool stops being evidential. The deeper limit, stated plainly: the Inspector audits a *snapshot* of particular models' outputs, not generative AI in general, and CLIP's demographic categories are blunt instruments for identities that are anything but.

## Walkthrough

A researcher enters "CEO" and then "nurse." The Inspector retrieves each profession's generated grid and CLIP-classifies the figures; the distribution charts and Void Reports make the asymmetry plain — one prompt skews overwhelmingly toward one gender and a narrow range of settings, the other toward another, with whole demographics absent. Switching on Dataset Alignment against `images/stable_bias/`, CLIP pairs the prompt with the closest images in the corpus, grounding the abstract finding in concrete material. Comparison mode juxtaposes the two professions so the contrast is unmissable.

## Critical Insight

The Imagination Inspector makes visible the boundaries of algorithmic imagination — not the technical ceiling of image synthesis but the *cultural* ceiling encoded in training data. By working with real generated images and foregrounding absence as much as presence, it converts a vague intuition that "AI is biased" into a documented, demographic-by-demographic account of what a model refuses to imagine. Generativity, the industry's term for fluent synthetic output, is held against creativity in the fuller sense — the capacity to imagine otherwise — and found wanting in a way the researcher can evidence and, via the suite's Contest tools, formally dispute.
